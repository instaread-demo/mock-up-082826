Anxiety in Children & Teens

by Karen Young (BSc)(Psych)(Hons)MastGestTher

The Art of Warm and Firm: How to hold the boundary without losing the relationship.

Every parent eventually finds themselves in the same impossible place.

Your child is yelling because you’ve said no.
They’re refusing to go to school.
They’re calling you names.
They’re begging, arguing, pleading, or shutting down.

And suddenly the question that arrives with unapologetic gusto and urgency is, ‘What do I do now?’

How do I hold the boundary without losing the relationship?

Do I get firmer? Softer?  

Do I hold the line? Or protect the relationship?

But parents know the hardest moments aren’t about choosing one or the other. They’re about trying to hold both at the same time.

Children don’t need adults who are either warm or firm. They need adults who are both, at the same time. Adults who can hold the child and the boundary at the same time.

The problem isn’t choosing one. It’s trying to hold both at the same time. The moments that test us most are rarely the calm ones. They’re the moments when your child is yelling because you’ve said no. When they’re refusing to leave the house to go to school, an exam, soccer. When they’re calling you names. When every part of you wants to either come down harder or give way completely.

What do children actually need from us?

Children need adults who can stay warm without becoming permissive. Firm without becoming frightening.

Adults who can hold the boundary and the child at the same time. Adults who can lead without controlling, connect without surrendering, and remain the steady when emotions are running high.

The thing nobody told us about boundaries

There’s a version of boundaries most of us grew up with that looked like one of two things: A wall, or nothing at all.

Either someone held the line so hard there was no warmth left on the other side of it, or they gave and gave until there was no line left to speak of. Many of us learned about boundaries from one of those two places – and most of us are still untangling it.

So when we talk about boundaries with our children, we bring all of that with us – the fear of being too hard, the guilt of saying no, the exhaustion of holding something that keeps getting pushed. We bring the version of boundaries we were given in our own childhoods, even when we don’t want to.

Boundaries aren’t just about behaviour.

The boundaries we set with our children aren’t just about managing behaviour in the moment. They’re teaching something much bigger. They teaching about what relationships are, about what love is allowed to look like, and about whether warmth and firmness can live in the same person at the same time.

What actually is a boundary?

A boundary isn’t punishment, and it isn’t a wall. It isn’t something we request from a child, and it isn’t something we do to them. It’s something we hold for them. 

A boundary doesn’t exist to control a child’s behaviour. It’s a statement of our behaviour, not a request for theirs. It exists to guide our own. It says:

This is what I’ll do to keep us safe. This is how I’ll protect our relationship. These are the values I’ll keep leading us towards. This is the line I’ll hold. This is how I’ll stay connected while I hold it. And this is how I’ll take care of you while you move through the feelings that boundary brings.

When we set a boundary, we’re not withdrawing love. We’re actually expressing it. We’re saying: I can see what you need right now, and I can also see what you need in the long run, and I love you enough to hold both of those things even when one of them is uncomfortable for us both.

That’s not cold, or harsh. It’s one of the most loving things a parent can do.

But here’s where it gets complicated. A boundary without warmth teaches a child that love goes cold when it gets firm. That to be corrected is to be distanced from. That the safest version of the person in front of them disappears a little when things get hard. That there’s no space for dissent without exile.

A boundary without firmness teaches something equally damaging – that the people who love us will eventually give in, that increasing their discomfort is a strategy that works, that relationships bend under enough pressure. This isn’t because the child is calculating or manipulative, but because children are exquisitely attuned to what works. They learn the system they’re given.

Neither is what we want to teach. Both happen when we’re tired, when we’re overwhelmed, when we’re running on the parenting patterns we absorbed before we were old enough to question them.

The two ways we lose the line.

When your child is distressed, their nervous system pulls on yours. Suddenly, every instinct tells you to protect them from the discomfort. You are wired to respond to their distress, and when holding the limit is the thing causing the distress, every instinct you have tells you to let it go, give in, make it stop.

So some of us bend. We say yes when we meant no. We walk back the consequence. We absorb the storm to restore the calm. Afterwards, we often feel the unease of it – that quiet sense that something was lost in the giving, that the line moved again, that we’re not quite sure who’s leading anymore.

Others of us hold – but we hold in a way that closes something down. The voice gets sharp. The warmth drains out. We stay firm but we go somewhere the child can’t reach us, and they feel that distance as much as they would have felt the consequence. They comply. But the relationship is fragile. Now we’ve positioned ourselves as someone to be avoided when things get messy, not someone to turn to. 

What we’re looking for is something harder than either of those. Something that requires us to stay regulated when everything in the moment is pulling us toward reactivity or retreat.

Why do children push against our bounaries?

Children don’t relax simply because we’re there. They relax because they sense that the relationship can hold them.

Children often push against boundaries not because they want them to disappear, but because they’re checking whether they’ll hold.

Think of a boundary as part of the container that holds a relationship. Children need to know where the edges are, and that those edges will stay steady even when they’re stretched by disappointment, anger, or protest.

When those edges disappear – when the adult gives in, retreats, negotiates away the limit, or hardens into anger -children rarely become calmer. Instead, they become less certain. More vigilant. More reactive.

Something in them starts searching again for the place where the relationship feels steady enough to rest.

Children don’t need to control the relationship.

They need to be able to rest inside it.

Hold the child and the boundary. 

The two parts of a healthy boundary.

The adult who is able to best lead, teach, and influence a child in meaningful, lasting ways is the one who is warm and strong. The one who is able to lovingly hold expectations, and provide the support the child needs to move towards those expectations. The one who is confident enough in the relationship to hold a limit without needing the child to be okay with it right now. Warmth + boundaries. Expectations + support.

That kind of relationship isn’t about control. It’s about being a safe enough, steady enough presence that the child doesn’t have to be in charge of what happens next.

When that presence is missing – when the adult shrinks, negotiates to the point of boundary erasure, or disappears into accommodation – children don’t relax. They escalate. Not because they want to be difficult, but because something in them is looking for the edge of the container and not finding it. The absence of a held limit doesn’t feel like freedom to a child. It feels like being alone at the wheel.

The most effective response.

The most effective parental response to difficult behaviour isn’t escalation and it isn’t capitulation. It’s presence. Calm, persistent, presence that doesn’t depend on compliance, obedience, or the child’s response. The parent who doesn’t need to win the argument in the moment but also doesn’t disappear from it. The one who announces clearly what they will and won’t accept, and then acts from that place – not with force, but with quiet, steady follow-through. 

Children need adults who are anchored. Who can be moved by their children’s feelings without being swept away by them. Who lead from warmth rather than fear, and hold from care rather than control.

Warm and immovable.

The goal isn’t a boundary that’s hard. It’s a boundary that holds and stays warm while it does.

This looks like a parent who can say no and mean it, without the no becoming cold. A parent who can hold a consequence without withdrawing. A parent who can stay present in the discomfort of their child’s disappointment, sadness, frustration, without either rescuing them from it or hardening against it.

It sounds simple. It isn’t. It requires a nervous system that’s regulated enough to stay connected under pressure. It requires enough self-awareness to notice when you’re about to give in from guilt or shut down from overwhelm. It requires having enough in your own tank that you can be fully present for something hard without running on empty.

That last part matters more than we often realise. A regulated parent isn’t a luxury. It’s the foundation everything else is built on. You cannot hold a warm boundary from a dysregulated nervous system. You can hold a cold one, or you can give in. But the steady, warm, immovable version of you requires something underneath it – rest, support, repair, enough moments of genuine restoration that you’re not running on fumes when things get hard.

What it actually sounds like

This is where it gets practical.

Because warm and firm is easy to understand, and much harder to do in the moment, especially when the moment is loud.

Here’s what it can look like.

Your child wants more screen time and starts to escalate when you say no.

Not this: Yelling or saying angrily, ‘I said no! End of story. I’m turning it off. Why do you have to do this every time!?’

This is a boundary without the warmth. The limit holds, but so does the distance. This is authority through force. The adult is in charge, but the child isn’t resting in them. They’re just beneath them.

Not this either: Ignoring them, or entering into an extended negotiation.

This is the warmth without the boundary. The relationship feels safe, but the line just moved. And something in the child registers that escalation works – not consciously, but in the way children always learn: through what happens next. Ultimately, this will often lead to an escalation where both the parent and child are yelling, or erasure where the parent disappears themselves, their expectations, or their needs to the points where the child is in control.

This: ‘I know you’re not done, and I get that’s really frustrating. We’re still turning it off now. I’ll sit with you for a bit while you settle.’

Or this: ‘I know this it’s annoying when it’s time to turn off. I’m going to sit right here beside you until it’s off.’ If they get angry, ‘I expected that you wouldn’t like it, and I’m not changing my mind.’

The limit is clear. The tone stays warm. You’re not arguing, over-explaining, threatening, or negotiating.

Not louder, not harder, just steadily there. Leadership through care, not control.

Your child says something hurtful when they’re dysregulated.

Not this: ‘Don’t speak to me like that! Go to your room right now.’

The limit might hold, or the child might get louder. Ultimately, the connection breaks. The child learns that when they are at their worst, the relationship contracts.

Not this: ‘I know you didn’t mean it. It’s okay.’ Connection holds, but the behaviour disappears into it. The child learns that the way they speak to people they love doesn’t really matter.

This: ‘I can hear how angry you are. I’m not going to listen when you’re speaking to me like that. When you’re ready to speak to me in a way I can hear, I’m right here. Take your time. You’re not in trouble.’

In both of these examples, the parent doesn’t match the child’s escalation, they don’t retreat from it, and they don’t pretend it didn’t happen.

They hold their position. And they hold the relationship. That’s what a boundary is – the steps we take to protect physical safety, relational safety, and the values we want them to move forward into the world with (such as kindness, respect, honesty, courage).

A child can only receive our guidance or our correction when they are oriented toward us. This is why our presence matters – warm and strong. During this, and our tone matters as much as the words.

A child who feels safe with you can feel corrected by you.
A child who doesn’t, can’t.

The script isn’t magic. They might still escalate. The boundary might still be hard to hold. But the presence you bring to it matters, because when your warmth doesn’t drain out as your firmness rises, your child learns something deeper than compliance. They learn:

My important adult can handle me.
The boundary is real.
The relationship is safe.
I don’t have to be in charge here.

That is what children rest in. And when children can truly rest in the care of their adults, they don’t need to spend as much energy scanning for safety, testing where the edges are, or trying to stay in control. That frees them for the work of childhood – to learn, to play, to think, to imagine, to connect, and to become.

What they’re learning.

Every boundary you hold is teaching your child something about relationships.

They’re learning whether love stays under pressure, such as when needs clash. Whether the people who care about them can disagree with them and still be present. Whether it’s safe to disagree, to share their thoughts, their opinions, or whether only one person can ever be right which means someone will always be wrong. Whether their distress has the power to move the people around them in ways that aren’t good for anyone.

You don’t have to be perfect for this. You just have to be present – warm enough that they feel safe with you, steady enough that they feel safe against you – enough times.

When things get messy, or when you say things you wish you didn’t or in ways that fell so far short of what you wanted – which will happen – now you have the opporutnity to model repair, to show them that you aren’t perfect and they don’t have to be either. This can be such a powerful part of their growth. ‘I’m sorry I yelled. That must have been confusing for you – me yelling at you to stop yelling. Can we try that again?’

The softest people can hold the firmest lines. The warmest voice can say the clearest no. The most loving thing you can do is stay – not just when it’s easy to, but in the discomfort, in the difficulty, in the full complexity that comes with relationships, and people.

And finally …

The goal isn’t to become a parent who never raises their voice. Or never gets it wrong. Or never has to repair.

The goal is something much simpler.

To become someone whose warmth doesn’t disappear when the boundary arrives. Someone whose strength doesn’t require fear. Someone whose love doesn’t retreat when things become difficult.

Every boundary teaches your child something. Not just about rules. About relationships. About whether love survives disagreement. About whether people can stay connected when needs collide. About whether strength and kindness can live in the same person.

Because children don’t rest in perfection.

They rest in adults who can hold them and the boundary at the same time.
