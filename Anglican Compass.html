St. Monica: The Mother Behind St. Augustine

By Anne Chester

St. Augustine, the priest, bishop, and arguably most influential theologian outside the Bible, is well known for his conversion story. His Confessions remain widely read and quoted today. But we would not know St. Augustine as we do without St. Monica, his mother, and her own life of Christian formation.

A Mother Formed in Faith

St. Monica was born around 331–332 A.D. in Thagaste, North Africa, in present-day Algeria. Born into a Christian family, she grew up in the faith. She married Patricius, a pagan man whom Augustine describes as having a volatile temper. Their marriage was not easy, but it helped form Monica’s faith. Augustine later wrote that God made his mother “reverently lovable and admirable” in her husband’s eyes (Confessions, IX.9). Near the end of his life, Patricius became a Christian, a conversion Augustine understood, at least in part, through Monica’s character and prayer.

St. Monica and Patricius had at least three children: Augustine, Navigius, and a daughter whose name Augustine does not give us. Monica committed herself to Christian formation in her home. She recognized Augustine’s intelligence early and supported his education. As a teenager, Augustine went to Carthage to study rhetoric.

After Patricius died, Augustine left home to pursue a life of pleasure and personal ambition. He rejected the Christian faith, embracing Manichaeism—a religious movement that claimed to offer answers to questions about good and evil.

As a mom, I cannot imagine the grief and panic Monica likely felt. Augustine was only around seventeen when he went to Carthage, still too young to fully count the cost of the life he was choosing. St. Monica committed herself to praying for her son’s conversion and pursuing him in hopes that he would return to the Christian faith.

Her pursuit shows formation in action. Each of us encounters opportunities for Christian formation—experiences that invite us to respond more faithfully to Christ. Formation shapes us; it isn’t simply a matter of making the correct choice. The question becomes: How will we respond, and who will we become through these opportunities?

Hope Without Control

St. Monica’s initial response when Augustine returned home was a boundary: she refused to allow him to live with her or eat at her table. Her formation included learning that she could love Augustine, pray for him, and pursue him, but she could not bring about his conversion. She desired something good for her son, but she did not control the outcome.

In her grief over Augustine’s rejection of the Christian faith, she receives a vision: she stands on a wooden rule. A radiant young man approaches and asks why she is sorrowful. When Monica says she grieves the loss of her son, he tells her to look and see Augustine standing beside her: “Where you are, there he is also.” Monica receives the vision as a promise that Augustine will one day stand with her in the Christian faith (Confessions, III.11).

Notice what God does not give her: no timeline for his conversion, no persuasive argument, and no plan for bringing him back. Monica receives something much harder to live with: hope without control.

When Persuasion Is Not Enough

It is easy to become scared and offended when our children come home with different, wrong, or harmful ideas. Children test our boundaries remarkably well. St. Augustine tries to turn his mother’s vision around, suggesting it may mean she will one day become a Manichaean. Monica immediately rejects his interpretation: the young man did not say, “Where he is, there you are,” but “Where you are, there he is also.”

St. Monica next tries persuasion. St. Augustine has his own gifts for persuasion and argument, and he remains unconvinced by the claims of Christianity. Monica turns persistently to local clergy, asking them to reason with Augustine. One bishop refuses her requests, encouraging her instead to leave Augustine alone for the time being and continue in prayer (Confessions, III.12).

Persuasion can influence behavior, but it cannot change the heart. The work of conversion belongs to the Holy Spirit alone. I often feel convicted while reading the Gospels by how differently people respond to the living Jesus standing before them. Persuasion does not eliminate choice.

When her attempts at persuasion fail, Monica doesn’t stop trying to influence the outcome. She stays close to Augustine.

From Pursuit to Surrender

When Augustine decides to leave Carthage for Rome, Monica invites herself along. However, Augustine has other plans and leaves her behind (Confessions, V.8).

When St. Monica discovers Augustine has left without her, she doesn’t respond quietly. Throughout his Confessions, Augustine describes his mother’s many tears over him; in modern language, we might call it an ugly cry. How often, as parents, do we show our own disappointment at loud emotions? In my experience, the child is exasperated at this point.

A Long Journey Toward Patience

Through sheer determination, Monica crosses the Mediterranean and eventually joins Augustine in Milan. Augustine, too, is being formed. In Milan, he begins attending church and listening to St. Ambrose preach. At first, Ambrose’s eloquence draws Augustine, but gradually he listens to the substance of his teaching. Ambrose’s interpretation of Scripture begins to answer objections Augustine had long held against Christianity. Yet Ambrose is a busy bishop and lacks time for the lengthy arguments Augustine desires.

Augustine’s gradual disillusionment with Manichaeism happens apart from Monica. By the time she reaches him in Milan, Augustine has already moved away from it.

In Confessions, Augustine tells Monica that although he is no longer a Manichaean, he has not yet become a Catholic Christian. Remarkably, she doesn’t react with the frantic intervention we see earlier. Augustine says she remains confident that before she dies, she will see him as a faithful Christian (Augustine, Confessions, VI.1).

By the time Monica reaches Milan, something in her response has changed. She exhibits a growing patience and surrender. Monica is learning that motherhood has limits.

Receiving What Is Given

When St. Monica arrives in Milan, she practices her faith as she did in Africa—taking food and wine to the shrines of martyrs. St. Augustine shares a remarkable exchange between his mother and the doorkeeper of the Milan shrine. When she attempts to continue this practice, the doorkeeper stops her and says Ambrose has prohibited it. St. Monica appears to receive the correction without personalizing it or becoming defensive. St. Augustine says his mother willingly gave up the practice when she learned Ambrose had forbidden it, for she loved him greatly (Confessions, VI.2).

As a mom and an Anglican convert, I too hope to have this level of humility and surrender when traditions and practices differ from what I know. Formation is about receiving what is given. Christian tradition is more than the customs we create for ourselves, such as cinnamon rolls on Christmas morning. We receive a faith we did not invent. Yet not every familiar Christian practice or custom—incense, particular forms of dress, or local devotional practices—is the faith itself. Monica was willing to discover the difference.

While Monica was being formed in surrender, Augustine approached his own surrender.

Grace Does Its Work

In 386, Augustine reaches a breaking point. In a garden with his friend Alypius, he hears a child’s voice repeating, “Tolle lege, tolle lege,” meaning “Take up and read.” Augustine returns to the Scriptures he had left with Alypius. He opens the book and reads the first passage his eyes fall upon, Romans 13:13-14. Augustine describes a sudden certainty, and his doubts begin to fall away (Confessions, VIII.12).

Monica does not witness her son’s conversion. After years of prayer, tears, persuasion, and pursuit, the moment she longed for happens beyond her presence and control. Augustine and Alypius go to Monica and tell her what happened. She rejoices and praises God.

Monica could not produce Augustine’s conversion—neither could argument, intellect, or even Ambrose. Unlike his father, Augustine does not appear to be persuaded by Monica’s piety and devotion. Over time, grace does its work, and Augustine responds.

Monica is formed by clinging to her faith while increasingly surrendering the outcome to God. Augustine is formed through the gradual loss of the certainties and answers he had relied on. Their paths toward submission and surrender differ, but both are being formed through circumstances they could not fully control.

Where You Are, There He Is Also

The final chapter of St. Monica’s life brings us full circle. At Easter in 387, St. Augustine was baptized. Augustine and Monica traveled to Ostia, a port city near Rome, where they planned to sail home to Thagaste.

A Promise Fulfilled at Ostia

While waiting in Ostia, Monica and Augustine stood together at a window overlooking a garden and talked about what comes after this earthly life. Together, they contemplated eternal life with God (Confessions, IX.10). In this conversation, Monica’s earlier vision is fulfilled: the son she had once grieved now stands beside her in the faith.

Shortly afterward, Monica fell ill. She relinquished her desire to be buried next to her husband in North Africa. It no longer mattered to her where she was buried. She asked only that her sons remember her at the Lord’s altar (Augustine, Confessions, IX.11).

This final exchange shows the depth of Monica’s formation. Her love for Augustine had not diminished, but she no longer needed to determine the outcome. The mother who had spent years pursuing, persuading, praying, and weeping could finally rest in what God had done.
