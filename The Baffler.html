Revenge of the Nerds

Moving fast and breaking democracy

The Nerd Reich: Silicon Valley Fascism and the War on Democracy by Gil Durán. Avid Reader Press/Simon & Schuster, 352 pages. 2026.

The tech boom of the past quarter century has been one of the greatest wealth creation events in human history. Our species’ addiction to bright lights, blather, and self-gratification, powered by Moore’s Law and the breakneck development of technology, has generated trillions of dollars—the bulk of which have gone to a few dead-eyed men with a penchant for three-quarter zips. Today, the concentration of wealth in the hands of the wealthiest Americans far exceeds that of the Gilded Age and only continues to grow. The likes of Elon Musk now command fortunes that outpace the gross domestic products of entire nations. At the same time, all this vaunted technological “progress” and profit have coincided with, if not driven, extreme political polarization and a catastrophic breakdown in the social contract.

While the tech sector was presumptively “liberal” during the Obama years and even during Trump’s first term, the opening years of this decade marked a sharp rightward shift among Silicon Valley’s elite. Where Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg once spoke of the importance of honesty and human connection, he now defends the AI-generated propaganda and content-trash that pollutes Facebook. Where Palantir once claimed its software protected civil liberties and free speech, its CEO Alexander Karp now regularly rants about “woke” threats to Western civilization while championing AI-driven military dominance. Where Musk was once a vocal champion of climate action, he’s recently spent his time wielding a chainsaw at the Department of Government Efficiency, consuming high-grade psychedelic drugs, and ranting about Third-World migration on a platform he reverse-engineered into a racist cesspool.

In San Francisco, the turn was particularly pronounced: Propelled by money structured in a matryoshka-doll set of interlocking “gray money” organizations, a segment of the tech elite launched successful recall campaigns in 2021 for activist San Francisco School Board members and the progressive District Attorney Chesa Boudin. Within two years, this network of donors and their associated megaphones succeeded in electing a swath of conservative, pro-market, pro-police politicians to office in the famously progressive city. But that was just the beginning: Soon, the likes of PayPal mafia alumnus David Sacks and serial angel investor Jason Calacanis of Sequoia Capital were backing Donald Trump’s third bid for the White House. In the 2024 election, the sheer volume of tech and crypto money gave the first felon to occupy the Oval Office a massive boost, with at least $273.2 million flowing from tech figures into Trump’s coffers, according to the Guardian.

That year, Gil Durán coined the sobriquet “Nerd Reich” to describe the pestilent mishmash of hard-right ideologies fueling the Bay Area’s reactionary turn. A former California political consultant and journalist who ran the opinion sections at the Sacramento Bee and was an editor at the San Francisco Examiner, Durán turns his attention to the full panoply of Silicon Valley’s authoritarian class in his new book. The portrait is grim. “Tech fascism is the product of a venture capital system that has run out of unicorns and must now hunt Antichrists to meet its unquenchable thirst for acceleration,” he writes. “Artificial intelligence companies hype the coming of a ‘godlike’ force capable of wiping out most jobs and, perhaps, all of humanity. Crypto companies peddle fantasies of digital salvation, shilling tokenized scams supposedly designed to survive the impending collapse of the US dollar and the liberal democratic order. . . . New crops of biotech start-ups promote delusional notions of eternal life while others launch the next generation of killer robot drones.”

Unlike most contemporary accounts of Silicon Valley, The Nerd Reich is not a biography, an inquest into a particular business, or a “true crime” accounting; it is an urgent intellectual history of a seemingly inchoate political moment. His project, which connects the rapacious search for profits with the quest for total control over our political, economic, and social lives, reveals that the fever dreams of our tech overlords are borne of a eugenics-friendly, totalitarian current that has long run underneath the surface in the high technology sector. The difference today is that the nerds are done pretending they are beholden to, or accountable to, anyone other than themselves.

While Durán’s account begins with Trump’s return to the White House, he spends most of his time on the San Francisco Peninsula and the wider Bay Area. In Durán’s telling, the Nerd Reich evolved from a series of university and tech publications—Peter Thiel’s The Stanford Review most notably—libertarian startups like PayPal, online forums, and a thread of libertarian ideology that evolved alongside the techno-optimist elements of the 1960s counterculture that proved so influential in the Bay Area.

Durán controversially, but accurately, posits figures like former Grateful Dead songwriter and Electronic Frontier Foundation cofounder John Perry Barlow as a cornerstone figure of this world through his endless promotion of “cyberlibertarianism.” He points to The Sovereign Individual: How to Survive and Thrive During the Collapse of the Welfare State, written by James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg, as deeply influential to Thiel and his coterie on the question of the obsolescence of democracy. More controversial ideas—including California’s own dark history of eugenics and Ayn Rand-influenced free market boosterism and anticommunism—are also correctly identified as critical touchstones in this intellectual movement.

What truly binds together this movement is a desire for freedom from, if not total control over, nation-states; a belief in the superiority, either innate or engineered, of a superior class of elite coders and engineers; and a complete rejection of any conception of social responsibility or equality under the law. “For many years, these ideas—that nations would collapse, that democracy would become obsolete, that a tech-powered elite would rule through technology—seemed like fringe science fiction fantasy to most observers,” Durán writes. “But they have now become the operating principles of some of the wealthiest and most powerful men in the world and have spawned a movement promoted by a rotating cast of venture capitalists, fringe philosophers, and political operatives who took these ideas from obscure blogs to the halls of power.”

Key to the Nerd Reich’s quest to usurp or secede from democratic life is the concept of network cities, developed by former Coinbase executive Balaji Srinivasan: for-profit, privately run enclaves outside of government control. The network city draws on a longer history of libertarian utopias but have been given new life by the likes of Próspera, a company seeking to build them around the world. Srinivasan, Thiel, and the investor Marc Andreessen are formal backers of Próspera.

One of the more infamous examples of the network city is California Forever. First exposed in 2023 after investors surreptitiously purchased so much land that national security warnings were raised about a possible Chinese espionage effort near Travis Air Force Base, it was envisioned as a ground-up urban development that would eventually house up to four hundred thousand people. Though the term “network city” was never used explicitly by California Forever’s backers, billionaire Michael Moritz did state that the new city would be a place where new forms of governance would be tested out. The project appeared to fall apart in 2024 once locals got wind of the proposed development and the grievous impact it would have on the local agriculture and the delta’s fragile ecosystems, though Jan Sramek, the project’s head, is still trying to develop the land into a shipyard and a defense manufacturing and tech hub. That proposal, however, is on decidedly thin ice.

Actors within the Trump administration are trying to set up similar “Freedom Cities” across the country, including in San Francisco’s Presidio and on an old naval air station in Alameda. It should come as no surprise that similar “charter cities” are being also proposed for Greenland and Gaza. As Durán argues, network cities encapsulate so many elements of the Nerd Reich’s ambitions: unchallenged political control, unregulated finance, and a monopoly on state repression and violence. Unfortunately, he fails to indict actors on both sides of the aisle for supporting efforts like this, including Colorado’s crypto-loving Democratic Governor Jared Polis.

Cryptocurrency is another key element of the Nerd Reich in that it provides the material goods to create an alternative financial system as well as a means to siphon wealth away from traditional institutions and outside government remit, directly challenging the state’s monopoly over monetary policy and financial regulation. Indeed, firms like the crypto exchange Coinbase were among the largest donors to the 2024 federal election cycle. “Crypto bought them an alliance with Trump, who had long derided the industry as a ‘scam’ but now became a champion of a Silicon Valley vision built on crypto, AI, war, and the network state,” Durán writes. Since Trump returned to office, the president has enriched himself to the tune of more $1 billion through cryptocurrency, pardoned industry executives convicted of financial crimes under prior administrations, and created a “strategic cryptocurrency reserve” backed by the United States government. Crypto provides a compelling case study of how the tech elite are rewriting the rules of influence, power and governance by buying the allegiance of politicians and remaking the apparatus of American governance into a smaller, more pliable apparatus.

Even as Big Tech rails against excess government spending, its fortunes are increasingly reliant on that very same government spending: from Musk’s SpaceX to Palantir’s massive federal tenders and the evolving “defense tech” universe that centers around Anduril, another Thiel-backed firm that builds autonomous drones and sensors. Its CEO, Palmer Luckey, is an enthusiastic participant in the Rockbridge Network, a political advocacy network founded by Vice President J. D. Vance and intended as a Trump-aligned alternative to the Koch network. Consider, too, Palantir. The company’s software provides the core of the Department of Homeland Security’s targeting software for use in Trump’s nationwide immigration raids that are becoming more widespread and deadly with each passing month. Similarly, the Pentagon has used Palantir analytics to provide bombing targets during the disastrous war with Iran, which among other atrocities resulted in the killing of at least 150 students in the city of Minab.

Of course, the true gorilla in the room is artificial intelligence, which is fueling a massive investment bubble while gutting Silicon Valley’s coding and engineering class. Almost a million tech employees have lost their jobs since 2020, and AI agents are now more efficient at coding than their human counterparts. Appropriately, Thiel protege and OpenAI’s CEO Sam Altman gets his turn in the sun. As Durán writes, Altman is “a man convinced of his own grand destiny to shape the world through venture capital,” and yet so paranoid about the potential for apocalyptic crisis that could be brought about in part through his own company’s development that he spends an inordinate amount of his free time prepping for survival and equipping his house with, according to The New Yorker, “guns, gold, potassium iodide, antibiotics, batteries, water, [and] gas masks from the Israeli Defense Force.”

The AI crowd is by far the most bizarre when it comes down to their motivating beliefs: Durán uses the obscure academic acronym TESCREAL (which stands for transhumanism, extropianism, singulatarianism, cosmism, rationalism, effective altruism, and long-termism) to describe them, which essentially boils down to eternal life and collective super intelligence through super-powerful computers, life-extension technology. For the Nerd Reich crowd, AI is an accelerant for their ascent to power, a financial cash cow, a means of production that (without government regulation or intervention) they will unilaterally control, and, depending on who you ask, the fulfillment of Ray Kurzweil’s Singularity prophecy. The bottom line is artificial intelligence is viewed by the tech elite as a means to cement their position at the summit of humanity, no matter how much harm it does to humanity. As Durán writes:

Consumer AI apps, the supposed saviors of the human race, are already being used to drive people into psychosis, talk children into suicide, and produce synthetic child sexual abuse material. Its masters promise it will also kill most of our jobs and replace human existence and experience with synthetic algorithmic realities. And all of it to create a fascist crypto casino that turns billionaires into trillionaires and turns ordinary people into their data-producing herd cattle.

When it comes to the Pentagon, readers may be shocked at the degree to which the government has handed over the reins to the AI companies: Durán details the create of “Detachment 201” in June 2025, which resulted in four executives from Meta, Palantir and OpenAI being inducted into the United States Army reserve at the rank of colonel. These firms are now essentially in charge of crafting American defense policy on artificial intelligence software, autonomous weaponry, and the future of warfare. The fox is truly in the henhouse.

Unfortunately, Durán leaves unexplored some of the more fringe, cultic elements of the Nerd Reich—such as the eugenics wing of the effective altruism movement and the Zizians, the homicidal splinter group whose members were involved in a series of killings across the United States in recent years. But that absence is more than made up for by the focus on the real-world consequences of an autocratic, bloodthirsty White House cozy relationships with companies like Palantir, whose executives freely quote Nazi philosopher Carl Schmitt and The Camp of the Saints author Jean Raspail, the author of a dystopian 1973 novel about European civilization crumbling under waves of Third World immigration that underpins the “Great Replacement” theory coined in the 2010s by French reactionary Renaud Camus. It is now a core belief of the transatlantic extreme right.

Still, The Nerd Reich is sometimes hampered by its ambition and scope. The sheer breadth of ideological movement and theories clustered under the Nerd Reich—from Christian Nationalism to the TESCREAL “bundle” to techno-libertarianism—all warrant more in-depth treatment. Durán’s account is a good introduction, but even as a reporter who’s both lived in the Bay Area and reported not only on the far right but on elements of the Nerd Reich, I was left wanting more detail than the capsule-sized components doled out at a breakneck pace in this book.

What does work to the book’s advantage is Durán’s methodical approach and consistency of perspective: he does not fall into the all-too-familiar trap of favoring access over accountability, a pitfall that has ensnared countless journalists down the years. Tech reporters are, even at this late stage, still vulnerable to this kind of starry-eyed boosterism, epitomized by Kara Swisher’s decades of gullible, industry-friendly copy and Jonathan Weber’s book-length insider account of the Valley, City on the Edge. The latter, published earlier this year, could have been a useful account of Silicon Valley’s radicalization over the past quarter-century that have turned it into a wealthier, crueler place. Instead, Weber’s fondness for recounting business deals, media inside baseball and the chummy elements of San Francisco’s famously incestuous and cutthroat politics leaves knowledgeable readers scratching their heads at the gaping holes in his account. That’s not the case with The Nerd Reich, which elucidates the higher-level architecture of what motivates the region’s wealthiest power players—and the grave stakes of the moment.

Despite the Nerd Reich’s growing influence in Washington and over the daily lives of most everyone on the planet, Durán is not a doomsayer. The book ends on a note of cautious optimism about the nationwide revulsion at the tech oligarchs and plutocracy writ large, arguing for the necessity of crypto bans, prosecutions, hearings, heavy AI regulation and most notably, formally recognizing “the network state and all similar anti-government schemes as what they are—extremist ideologies designed to foment sedition and societal harm.”

The foundations of our latest tech boom are also fragile. AI is far from profitable at the moment as capital inputs and trade disruptions triggered by the current administration’s schizophrenic trade and foreign policy approaches repeatedly spooks markets. The bubble may still well burst, and Durán highlights financial analysts’ concerns about the over-leveraged financial structure of AI companies, and a “chasm between investment and revenue” that “makes the AI gold rush an extremely high-stakes gamble.” Meanwhile, Americans of all stripes are livid at the breakneck expansion of data center development to feed the AI boom, with protests against new developments springing up in red and blue states alike almost as rapidly as the tech firms’ need for new energy sources. Seventy percent of all Americans, including Democrats and Republicans, oppose data center construction in their community, according to a recent Gallup poll. From Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan to Melat Kiros in Colorado, politicians once deemed too left for popular consumption are running highly competitive campaigns largely due to their focus on data center development.

Not only that: Thiel, Musk, Altman and their ilk are deeply foreign to much of the United States, not only by dint of their wealth but the outlandish collection of ideologies that motivate their behavior. “The tech cult offers a buffet of bizarre ideas that seemed designed to terrify normal people—especially Americans who believe in God and see their country as a divinely anointed land that will exist forever,” Durán writes. That’s leaving aside the development of Terminator-style killer robots and job-killing artificial intelligence that no one except for the investment class seems to want.

And that’s the ultimate question here: If the Nerd Reich’s vision of a new social order where they hold the power is truly going to come to pass, or if a popular upsurge can bring them to heel.  The sheer tenor and speed of the anti-data center uprisings this year are remarkable, and may prove decisive in this November’s midterm elections, where the balance of power could begin to shift away from the Nerd Reich.
