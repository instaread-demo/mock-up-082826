Rebel Creamery Files Bankruptcy After Losing $23.8 Million Ice Cream Fight

Some shoppers grabbed the wrong pint and never noticed. A federal judge noticed. Rebel Creamery, a keto ice cream brand sold at Walmart, Kroger and Safeway, has filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Utah. The filing comes weeks after a court ordered Rebel to hand over $23.785 million to rival Van Leeuwen Ice Cream for copying its pint design. Rebel calls the fight unfinished. It is appealing the ruling while asking a bankruptcy court for protection from creditors.

The numbers show how deep the hole is. Rebel listed about $13.78 million in assets against $23.85 million in liabilities, according to bankruptcy filings in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah. That gap means the company owes more than it owns, mostly because of one debt: the Van Leeuwen judgment. Court records show Rebel’s cash, receivables and inventory add up to about $13.5 million total, far short of covering the disputed claim it is fighting in appeals court.

Rebel is not backing down from the underlying case. The company filed a notice of appeal on Aug. 12, then sought bankruptcy protection two days later. Its own paperwork lists the Van Leeuwen claim as disputed, not settled. But the judge who heard the case did not describe a close call. He found that Rebel copied Van Leeuwen’s packaging on purpose, which raises a harder question: how does packaging alone bring down a national grocery brand?

A Federal Judge Found Rebel Copied Van Leeuwen’s Packaging on Purpose

Trade dress law protects the way a product looks, not just its name or logo. Van Leeuwen’s pints use a plain cardboard container, matching lids, soft pastel colors and black script lettering, a look the company spent years developing. Judge Eric Komitee found that Rebel’s packaging copied that combination closely enough to confuse shoppers. That finding, not just the sales numbers, is what turned a design dispute into a $23.8 million verdict.

The evidence at trial went beyond expert opinion. Store employees mixed up the two brands, shelving them together and tagging Rebel pints with Van Leeuwen’s price. Shoppers made the same mistake at home, according to letters entered as trial evidence. U.S. District Judge Eric Komitee summed it up plainly, writing that “Rebel infringed and diluted Van Leeuwen’s trade dress and did so intentionally.”

None of this happened by accident, the court found. Rebel’s founders, Austin and Courtney Archibald, designed their packaging themselves in Adobe Illustrator, without any background in graphic design. Van Leeuwen, by contrast, hired the design firm Pentagram in 2016 and tested seven concepts before choosing one. The distance between those two processes turned out to matter less than the finished product on the shelf, which is where the real damage shows up.

Shoppers, Cashiers and Clerks All Made the Same Mistake

The mix-ups were not rare. Multiple grocery store employees put Rebel’s price stickers on Van Leeuwen pints and stocked the two brands side by side, according to trial evidence. A customer described sending her husband out for one brand and watching him return with the other. She said a friend on the opposite side of the country reported the identical mistake, which suggested the confusion was not a one-off.

Timing mattered to the court’s findings. Van Leeuwen redesigned its packaging in August 2016. Over the next two years, its annual growth rate jumped from about 35.8% to 91.6%, according to figures cited in the court’s order. Rebel launched more than a year later, in September 2017, and its pints reached store shelves by August 2018. A Van Leeuwen employee spotted the resemblance within months.

By the time the case reached trial, the paper trail was long and one-sided. Van Leeuwen kept every early sketch and rejected design from its 2016 process, evidence its design firm produced in court. Austin Archibald testified that Rebel saved only the finished packaging file, with no earlier drafts. That gap between one company’s careful records and the other’s missing history left the court with little room for doubt, and it set up a much bigger problem than a lawsuit.

The Judgment Rebel Disputes Is Bigger Than Rebel Itself

Bankruptcy does not erase the debt Rebel is fighting. Chapter 11 pauses collection while the company reorganizes, but the $23.785 million claim from Van Leeuwen still sits on Rebel’s books as its single largest liability. Rebel disputes the claim and has a pending appeal, yet the filing itself shows how much weight one court ruling can carry. A company that stocks shelves at Walmart and Kroger nationwide is now asking a bankruptcy judge for room to survive.

Court filings do not say the Van Leeuwen judgment alone caused the bankruptcy. Rebel listed liabilities and assets each in the $10 million to $50 million range, a wide estimate that points to other financial pressure beyond the lawsuit. Still, the math is hard to ignore: the disputed judgment is worth more than everything Rebel owns. Bankruptcy attorney Michael Johnson of Ray Quinney & Nebeker is representing the company as it works through both fights at once.

Rebel still calls the judgment disputed. Its own paperwork says so, and the appeal is real. But bankruptcy filings do not wait for appeals to finish, and neither do grocery store shelves. Komitee’s order does not just cost Rebel money: it strips away the exact packaging that helped Rebel’s ice cream sell next to Van Leeuwen’s in the first place. The debt is what pushed Rebel into court. The redesign is what may decide whether shoppers ever find its pints again.
