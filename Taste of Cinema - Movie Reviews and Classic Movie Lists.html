10 Blatant Movie Rip-Offs That Are Actually Fantastic

It’s a given that studios often don’t know what will be a success until audiences have spoken and voted with their wallets. Then, whatever is popular, you can bet executives will get their chequebooks out and make as much of it as humanly possible. We saw the ‘slasher’ craze take over the industry once “Halloween” hit big in 1978, while we’re still being flooded with superhero movies after “X-Men” and “Spider-Man” broke records in the early 2000s. A certain type of movie, fitting a certain genre, will take over the release schedule until audiences grow tired of it and it stops making bank.

Yet, there are even more blatant attempts at copying a trend: films that veer so close to the formula they’re inspired by that you could easily call them knock-offs, clones, or rip-offs. Most of the time, these are films whose sole purpose is to eat a meal off the plate of a much better or more popular movie and ride its success towards a quick payday. More often than not, that’s exactly what they are – but sometimes, just sometimes, the resulting movie can actually be surprisingly strong on its own.

From big-budget Hollywood clones to low-budget Roger Corman rip-offs, let’s explore films that were clear imitators of highly regarded blockbusters but still managed to be surprisingly watchable and good in their own way.

 

1. The Taking of Beverly Hills (1991) – Die Hard (1988)

When “Die Hard” launched in cinemas in 1988, it took the action scene by storm and made TV star Bruce Willis an immediate A-lister, in a move that was unprecedented at the time. Throughout the ’90s, the concept became a trademark formula for blockbusters, with several big stars doing variations on it over the years; Stallone’s “Cliffhanger” was ‘Die Hard on a mountain’, Seagal’s “Under Siege” was ‘Die Hard on a boat’, and Keanu Reeves’s “Speed” was ‘Die Hard on a bus’. One movie, though, tried quite quickly and blatantly to ride its coattails and was dismissed as a carbon copy, even though it actually came closest to capturing the first movie’s essence perfectly.

“Beverly Hills” spun the formula from an isolated office building to an entire L.A. neighbourhood held under siege by corrupt cops and mercenaries headed by the suitably sinister Robert Davi, with only unlikely hero Ken Wahl, teaming up with Matt Frewer’s bumbling cop, to take down the bad guys.

Unlike later spins on the formula, which simply swapped locations, “Hills” attempts to really replicate what made the film so special, from its mix of hard R-rated bloodletting with light-hearted wisecracking, to its satirical jabs at authority and bureaucracy, to even attempting to make Wahl, then well-known on TV’s “Wiseguy”, an explosive superstar like Willis. Audiences caught on and rejected the film, which is a grand shame because it is an absolute blast.

Wahl is charming and believable in the main role, good with the quips, handsome enough for the ladies, and strong enough when it comes to the action. His pairing with Frewer and their off-kilter chemistry really holds the film together and has you rooting for the two. Davi (a Die Hard alumnus) was one of the stronger action villains of this era, and he doesn’t disappoint, playing his bad guy as strangely sympathetic, a man scorned by love and now ruthlessly going for his objective.

The entire spectacle is handled with the deft hand of Sidney J. Furie (“The Ipcress File”), a criminally underrated genre director who could knock out slick action thrillers on zero budget, and here he makes the movie feel just as big and exciting as the movie it is clearly aping. All in all, it makes for a must-watch for action fans.

 

2. Carnosaur (1993) – Jurassic Park (1993)

From the 50s to the 90s, there was no filmmaker prouder to wear the title of ‘rip-off’ king than Roger Corman. He had a long history of knocking out quick titles to feed off the hype of bigger movies; usually, the films were tepid cash-ins, but sometimes (as seen later in this list), they actually managed to be quite enjoyable send-ups. “Jurassic Park” took the world by storm in 1993, and Corman had been holding onto the rights to a dinosaur-related pulp novel, published in 1984, since 1991. Seeing the opportunity, he fast-tracked production in order to beat Spielberg’s classic adventure movie to screens with an enjoyably madcap dino pic.

Set in the isolated deserts of the American Southwest, the plot features Diane Ladd’s insane scientist unleashing contaminated poultry products into civilisation, resulting in a pandemic of females impregnated with strange creatures, and a vicious genetically engineered dinosaur violently murdering anything that stands in its path.

‘Park’ was one of the biggest event movies of the early ’90s, a horror-influenced thrill ride that could be viewed by the whole family; Corman’s variation is very much hard-R, scuzzy, and stomach-churningly gory. It caters to gorehounds and actually features a bevy of impressive practical effects by SFX maestro John Carl Buechler. Of course, it’s not on the level of the blockbuster it apes, but considering its budget, the dinosaur attacks are surprisingly effective, with some strong action set pieces to boot.

It helps that the movie is helmed by genre underdog Adam Simon (“Brain Dead”); he manages to direct the scenes slickly with a tinge of suspense, but also infuses proceedings with an off-kilter sense of humour, no doubt fuelled by its nonsense plot. Ladd’s scientist is in on the joke, but still manages to give the character a frightening, dark ruthlessness, whilst genre stalwarts like Clint Howard and Ned Bellamy do their best to steal their scenes. The main duo of Raphael Sbarge and Jennifer Runyon are fairly vanilla, but their arcs are paid off in a shocking, downbeat final scene that will flabbergast viewers.

All in all, it was a blatant rip-off of a much better movie, but it has enough style, surprises, and decent effects to be an enjoyable watch on its own merits, not to mention it is arguably more charming and inventive than some of the later ‘Jurassic’ sequels.

 

3. Salute of the Jugger (1989) – Mad Max 2: The Road Warrior (1981)

George Miller’s classic “Mad Max” spread ‘Ozploitation’ internationally and gave Mel Gibson his breakthrough performance, but it was its sequel, “The Road Warrior”, that created the iconic post-apocalyptic landscape that is still etched into our collective idea of what the end of the world looks like cinematically. Several movies attempted to rip off the same type of minimal plot and aesthetic, from big-budget (“Waterworld”) to low-budget (“Steel Dawn”); all came up short in matching the Australian-set inspiration. However, one often-overlooked film copied the world almost verbatim, but told a completely different type of story within it.

In a future where civilisation has crumbled, a group of survivors, led by Rutger Hauer’s gruff leader, are Juggers, skilled travelling players of a sport that makes “Rollerball” look sanitised. As they move from town to town, Joan Chen’s determined protagonist finds purpose as a new member, whilst they train to challenge the elite League of the sport.

“Jugger” lifts Miller’s world almost wholesale; shot in the Australian desert by the first “Mad Max” DoP, David Eggby, it features a bleak environment where survivors wear makeshift armour and have sand-worn faces – and yet, it’s a rough-and-ready sports movie, and one unlike any other that comes to mind.

Hauer is fantastic as the brittle alpha leader, ruthless yet charismatic; it’s a performance that deserves to sit next to his other peak international performances of the time, with Chen holding her own as the green yet fierce upstart who gets taken under his wing. The rest of the crew is filled with several fantastic character actors, such as Vincent D’Onofrio and Delroy Lindo, all embellishing the setting nicely.

That said, what truly elevates the film is the talent behind the camera, namely legendary screenwriter David Webb Peoples (“Unforgiven”, “12 Monkeys”, “Blade Runner”) making his sole directorial effort. The story, as is his trademark, is airtight and engaging; a genre world filled with three-dimensional beings. But he also directs the film with strong confidence for a first stab, with brutal and bloody game matches set in an impressively bleak, hellish world that really impress. Yet these characters manage to find a smidgeon of hope to fight towards, in what becomes a surprisingly human tale.

 

4. Piranha (1978) – Jaws (1975)

Steven Spielberg undoubtedly changed the film landscape forever in the ’70s with “Jaws”; at a point where experimental character pieces and hard-boiled thrillers were the most appealing film fodder, his exhilarating monster movie took hold of audiences and essentially created the modern summer blockbuster. So, of course, everyone and their mother wanted to create a similar product, from cheap rip-offs (Tentacles) to Hollywood repeats (Orca), all failing to come close. Surprisingly, one of the best was Roger Corman’s attempt, replacing a giant shark with vicious Amazonian fish, and it even had Spielberg himself singing its praises.

Summertime in rural Texas, and something is not right in the water. After a few random people get taken down in grisly attacks, Heather Menzies’ investigator teams with Bradford Dillman’s recluse to get to the bottom of things, with their findings leading to the revelation that genetically enhanced piranhas are loose in the lakes, after escaping a military facility.

Plot-wise, it ticks all the same tropes as “Jaws”: a shocking opening attack, creepy underwater POVs, and authorities who ignore the protagonists’ warnings in the name of big business – yet it does so with a sly knowing nod to its classic inspiration, not attempting to outdo it, but to be a fun variation on proceedings. It helps that the main duo of Dillman and Menzies are enjoyable characters who share enough chemistry and chops to make them engaging as leads. It’s also filled to the brim with appearances from genre legends like Kevin McCarthy, Barbara Steele, Paul Bartel, and Keenan Wynn.

Being a late-’70s Corman production, behind the scenes it is chock-full of future Hollywood players, from future big-name Joe Dante (The Howling) making his solo feature directorial debut, indie auteur John Sayles (Lone Star) handling the scripting, future SFX legends Rob Bottin and Phil Tippett working in the effects department, and even renowned genre producer Jon Davison (RoboCop) producing. With this high level of talent, of course the movie is a watchable product, with Spielberg being so impressed with Dante’s movie that they later collaborated on “Twilight Zone: The Movie”, before Dante being personally offered the job on what would become his A-list breakthrough movie, “Gremlins”.

 

5. Demons (1985) – The Evil Dead (1981)

Sam Raimi’s low-budget horror thrillfest, “The Evil Dead”, took the movie world by storm. Filmed on a shoestring budget, infused with a subversive and manic style, and with a mandate to shock and gross out audiences at all costs, it pretty much created the setup of ‘kids go to a cabin in the woods’ as a jumping-off point for countless horror movies since. It must be said, too, it managed to create a movie monster species that was completely original, terrifying and darkly comic: the Deadites. Evil spirits possessing hapless humans, playing like a mix of Linda Blair’s Regan and a Romero zombie, they were unique creatures that were so infused into the film’s thematics that many filmmakers have not bothered to try and replicate them, aside from this gonzo Italian horror concocted by the dream-team of Lamberto Bava and Dario Argento.

Set in ’80s West Berlin, a bunch of random civilians get invited to a mysterious horror movie screening by a masked weirdo. Locked inside the theatre, the movie-within-the-movie is about a haunted mask that turns unsuspecting humans into demonic hosts, with it coming to real life as audience members become possessed and turn into monstrous beings as well.

The plot is fun and quite original, even if the one-note characters are basically gore fodder. But the ‘demons’ that torment the cinema are very much ripped out of Raimi’s classic movie, albeit with fewer one-liners and a penchant for being even more sadistic. Regardless, it feels less like a copy and more like an original spin on the other film’s template due to its unique setting (with one particular sequence even being homaged in “Scream 2” later on). And in a weird twist, its lesser sequel, “Demons 2”, placed the possession pandemic in an urban high-rise, which ended up becoming the setting for “Evil Dead Rise”, in a coincidental turn that came full circle.

Argento, as co-writer/producer, is game to push for a movie that’s gross, suspenseful and full of wild plot swerves. This is also probably one of Bava’s strongest films, with his other productions lower on the quality scale compared to his ’80s Italian directing peers; the movie looks slick and colourful, and he’s able to ratchet up the tension nicely before an apocalyptic third-act escalation turns everything into fun, bloody chaos, featuring an incredible motorcycle/samurai sword rampage fueled by a heart-pounding soundtrack filled with heavy metal greats (e.g., Mötley Crüe).
