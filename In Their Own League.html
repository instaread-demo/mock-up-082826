Review: It Ends

Year: 2025

Runtime: 89 minutes

Director/Writer: Alex Ullom

Actors: Mitchell Cole, Akira Jackson, Noah Toth, Phinehas Yoon

By Taylor Hunsberger

Alex Ullom’s debut feature “It Ends” (2025) is out this week from Neon and represents a new kind of dreadful and claustrophobic horror that manages to maintain its sense of wonder and tenderness. What begins as what I can only describe as a bombardment drives its way (literally and figuratively) into an existential adventure like I’ve never seen before.

College friends Tyler (Mitchell Cole), Day (Akira Jackson), Fisher (Noah Toth), and James (Phinehas Yoon) are carpooling home for a break on a long winding road as the sky grows dark. Suddenly driver Tyler comes upon a dead end and makes the realization that he has not made a single turn in miles. Out of the dark comes a mysterious, violent force (I don’t want to spoil what it is! That’s all in the fun!). The crew run back in the car, slam the doors, and drive as fast as possible. It’s not long until they realize that they must keep driving or they will be attacked once more by the outside forces. Along the way, their friendships are tested, new routines for their new life are built, and they all must contemplate what it means when a journey seems unending and perhaps meaningless.

“It Ends” is a devastating coming of age story framed as a horror film. As the crew of friends develop their own routines to pass the time in their car that no longer runs out of gas in this world they’ve discovered, they discover how they all choose to cope with the inevitable unpredictability of the future. Some of the friends last longer than others, with some choosing to give up and give themselves over to whatever lurks outside. Others keep going and insist on chugging along until they find anything out there. 

The result is an extremely real presentation of the experience of getting ready to leave college and go out into the real world. Each of the friends makes use of their unending time in the car in their own way that gives this life meaning. After some scientific experiments to determine how long they can stay outside the car without being caught (90 seconds), Fisher and Day make a ritual of screaming into the void, the only real release they have in this environment. They make up games to play together as they drive, they develop a driving schedule, they find a way to start keeping track of the date and time. 

Ullom has found a way to make a viewer feel as trapped and anxious as his characters. I felt increasingly restless the longer the drive went on, unable to really imagine what could come next. But there comes a point when we and the characters wonder: maybe nothing ever will. And whether it does or does not is not exactly the point of the drive. It’s perhaps the journey and what these characters make of it, as they say.

Gorgeously shot through a forest both horrifying and beautiful thanks to cinematographers Evan Draper and Jazleana Jones, “It Ends” immerses its audience in the hills and valleys of life. Every performance is outstanding, particularly from Yoon who plays the stubborn yet determined James. I do not even know how the team was able to film the car in a way that pulls off the illusion that this road goes on forever. Ullom has created a stunning debut that expands what the horror genre can do.
