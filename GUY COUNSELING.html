How To End an Extramarital Affair with a Coworker

August 25, 2026 Jack Heart Ask Jack, Dating, Relationships

You’ve been having an extramarital affair a coworker

HIS QUESTION

Dear Jack,

I never thought I would admit this but here it goes. I’ve been having an extramarital affair with a coworker for the past two months. My wife and I have only been married for three years and I really do love her.

My coworker and I realize what we’re doing is wrong, yet we continue to sleep together. To complicate matters, she’s recently married too. For what it is worth, our attraction seems to be more physical than anything else – not that it matters.

I’m desperate for help because if my wife finds out, my marriage is over. I don’t want to lose her. How do I stop the affair?

-Confused Cheater

HIS ANSWER

Hi, CS,

It sounds like you’ve gotten yourself into quite a pickle. I can imagine this is a deeply confusing time, filled with mixed emotions.

On the one hand, you’re carrying the crushing guilt of knowing you’re stepping out on your wife, all the while professing your fidelity and recognizing it’s a complete lie.

On the other hand, there’s a part of you caught up in this other woman, feeling a rush of energy whenever the two of you are alone – in the biblical way.

The hard truth is office affairs happen more than most people would guess. They tend to involve people who are already married, and that’s no accident. There’s a reason the term workplace spouse made it into the American lexicon. Researchers who study infidelity point to a fairly simple mechanism behind it: proximity plus repetition plus shared stress builds intimacy whether you’re trying to build it or not. You spend eight, nine, ten hours a day with this person. You see them solve problems, crack under deadlines, laugh at the same dumb things you laugh at. That’s not an excuse – but it explains why this keeps happening to otherwise decent, otherwise faithful people.

But back to your actual question – how do you stop it?

I’m going to be direct with you and share some advice you probably don’t want to hear. But you didn’t come to Ask Jack for feel-good nonsense. You came for something you can actually use.

Step one: end the affair now, and do it in person.

Skip the text message, the email, the vague “we should talk” Slack DM. That approach is weak, and it leaves the door cracked open for a slow fade instead of a clean break. Ask to speak to her somewhere outside the office – a park, a coffee shop, anywhere away from the ears of coworkers – and go in with your message already decided. Not “I think we should maybe cool it off.” Something closer to: “This has to stop completely, starting today, and I need us both to mean it.”

When you have that conversation, don’t make it about assigning blame. You own just as much responsibility for this as she does. She may get upset, maybe even angry, particularly if her feelings run deeper than yours do. From what you’ve described, it sounds like the pull between you is mostly physical – which in some ways makes the ending easier, and in other ways makes it harder to trust that it’ll actually stay ended, because physical chemistry doesn’t care that you’ve had a conversation about boundaries.

Step two: build in structural distance, not just willpower.

Willpower alone rarely wins against two people who work fifteen feet apart and already know how to find a reason to be alone together. If you don’t share a department and barely interact, you’re in decent shape – distance does a lot of the work for you. But if your jobs put you in regular contact, you need actual guardrails: no more staying late together, no more solo lunches, no more volunteering for the same projects. If that’s not realistic given how your teams are structured, it may be worth quietly exploring a transfer, a schedule change, or even a new job. That sounds extreme until you remember the alternative is staying in daily arm’s reach of the one relationship most likely to blow up your marriage.

CS, let me tell you a story. Years ago, I was seriously dating someone – under the explicit banner of exclusivity – while also sleeping with a coworker. At one point, she came to me and ended it herself, in no uncertain terms. “The last time we slept together was our last,” she told me.

Except it wasn’t.

The pull was strong enough that it happened again. And again. It didn’t stop because either of us developed better discipline – it stopped because she took a job in another state and the physical distance did what our intentions couldn’t. I’m not proud of that, but I’d rather tell you the truth than a tidier version of it. If the two of you are still working in close range, one honest conversation may not be enough. You may need to remove the opportunity, not just the intention.

Step three: figure out whether you tell your wife – and don’t rush that decision.

This is the part guys always want a straight answer on, and I won’t pretend there’s one that fits every marriage. There are real arguments on both sides. Confessing can rebuild trust on honest terms, but it can also detonate a marriage that might have survived quietly, especially if the affair is already over and unlikely to repeat. Staying silent can protect the relationship in the short term, but it means carrying a secret that tends to leak out sideways – through guilt, distance, or an accidental slip years later that lands worse than the original affair would have. Some men process this with a therapist first, specifically to get honest about their own motives before deciding whether disclosure is about clearing your conscience or actually serving the marriage. Those aren’t always the same thing, and it’s worth knowing which one you’re doing before you open that conversation with her.

Step four: get honest with yourself about why this happened.

This is the step guys skip because it’s uncomfortable, and it’s the one that actually prevents a repeat. Was the marriage already under strain and this filled some gap? Was it purely opportunity and chemistry, nothing deeper going on at home? Were you looking for a version of validation you’re not currently getting, or giving yourself, anywhere else? I can’t answer that for you – nobody can from the outside – but sitting with those questions honestly, ideally with a therapist once the affair itself is behind you, will tell you more about whether you’re a guy who made a serious mistake or a guy who’s going to be back in this same spot in two years with someone else.

There’s an old saying that a leopard never changes its spots – once a cheater, always a cheater. Maybe there’s some truth in that for some people. But we also know that infidelity touches a large share of marriages in one form or another, and plenty of those men never repeat it. Which one you become isn’t decided by this affair. It’s decided by what you do starting today.

If you want this to stop, it will stop. You’re not helpless here. But if you let it continue, understand what you’re signing up for: at some point it comes out. Maybe not this month, maybe not this year, but eventually. And when it does, it won’t be a quiet conversation – it’ll be the kind that reshapes everything.

CS, I’m not here to pile on. I’ve got no room to lecture, and honestly, neither do a lot of men reading this if they’re being straight with themselves. We could spend all day talking about monogamy and how hard it actually is in practice. But you didn’t write in for a philosophy discussion – you wrote in for a way out. I’d point you toward I Cheated: Here’s What To Do After Having an Affair. It may not map onto your exact situation, but it’s close enough to help you think more clearly about your own.

You said you truly love your wife. I believe you. The real question isn’t whether you love her – it’s whether you love her enough to make the harder choice today, tomorrow, and the next time this coworker is standing a little too close.

Only you can answer that.
